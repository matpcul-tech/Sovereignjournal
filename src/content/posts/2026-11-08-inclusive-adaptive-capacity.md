A Commonwealth Fund report published this week found that community health centers using AI ambient scribing are cutting documentation time and letting clinicians spend more minutes with patients. The centers making it work fastest share one trait: they involved medical assistants, front-desk staff, and patient advocates in the rollout before the tool went live.

That detail is easy to skip past. It shouldn't be.

## The ceiling problem

Inclusive Adaptive Capacity is one of the core constructs in Adaptive Inclusive Leadership Theory. The plain version: an organization's ability to adapt is bounded by how widely it included voices before it had to adapt.

Not during. Before.

This is counterintuitive to most leaders under pressure. When something needs to change fast, the instinct is to tighten the circle, speed up the decision, and push it out. And you can do that. It moves fast in month one. By month six, you are spending everything you saved on resistance, workarounds, and the quiet exodus of the people who saw it coming and weren't asked.

The community health centers that widened the circle first didn't do it because they were patient or idealistic. They did it because a medical assistant knows things about workflow that no implementation consultant will ever surface in a needs assessment. A front-desk clerk knows why a patient hesitates at the door. That knowledge has nowhere to go if the circle is small.

When the AI tool landed, those centers had already processed the fear, negotiated the edge cases, and built the informal protocols that make formal systems actually function. The tool adapted to the room, not the room to the tool.

## Why this is structural, not cultural

Leaders hear "widen the circle" and file it under culture work. That's a category error.

Inclusive Adaptive Capacity is structural. It is about where information lives before a decision gets made. If your decision-making process only reaches people with formal titles, you have a structural bottleneck. The crisis doesn't create the bottleneck. The crisis reveals it.

In sovereign and rural communities, this bottleneck has a specific shape. The people closest to the problem, the community health worker doing home visits on a gravel road, the tribal enrollment clerk who fields the questions no form anticipated, are often the furthest from the meeting where the solution gets designed. The organization adapts without them, which means it adapts partially.

Partial adaptation works until the problem doesn't fit the partial picture. In rural health, it usually doesn't fit.

## What STAT's skeptics are actually saying

STAT ran a piece this week noting that some rural health leaders are skeptical of AI's promises. Trump officials say AI will save rural health care. The leaders in the field are less sure.

The skeptics are not wrong about technology. They are describing an inclusion failure. The AI tools being promoted at the policy level were largely designed with and for different settings. When a rural clinic tests them, the gaps show up fast: poor audio in older exam rooms, documentation templates built for urban specialty practices, patient populations whose first language isn't matched by the model.

That's not an AI problem. That's a circle problem. The people who built the tool didn't include the people who would use it in a forty-year-old clinic on a two-lane highway. The tool adapted to a room that doesn't exist.

The community health centers in the Commonwealth Fund report worked differently. They didn't wait for a perfect tool. They included local voices in shaping how the tool landed. That's the difference between borrowing someone else's adaptation and building your own.

## The practical test

If you are a leader in a sovereign nation, a rural health system, or any organization that is about to adopt something new, here is the question that matters: who is not in the room right now who will have to live with this decision?

Name them. Not the category. The person.

If you can't name them, your Inclusive Adaptive Capacity has a ceiling you can't see yet. You will find it at the worst possible time, when the pressure is highest and the margin for error is lowest.

Widen the circle before the crisis. The organizations that adapt fastest are the ones that did.

Pick up enough crumbs, you get a loaf of bread.
