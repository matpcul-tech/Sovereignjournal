GitHub's "Awesome" lists are breaking down. Maintainers built them alone, curated them alone, and now they're aging out faster than any one person can keep up. The community that relied on them never had a hand in them. So the lists drift, and trust goes with them.

That is not a software problem. That is a sensemaking problem.

## What participatory sensemaking actually means

In Adaptive Inclusive Leadership Theory, participatory sensemaking is the idea that meaning is made collectively. Not democratically, in the sense that every voice controls every outcome. Collectively, in the sense that the people closest to a problem help construct the frame before a decision locks in.

The distinction matters. A council that votes on everything moves slowly and badly. A council that asks the room before it decides moves slowly once and well for a long time.

Decisions made *with* the room hold longer than decisions made *for* the room.

This is not folk wisdom dressed up in theory language. It is a testable claim. When people participate in constructing the meaning of a problem, they carry the solution differently. They troubleshoot it. They defend it to skeptics. They flag when it starts to drift. When people receive a solution handed down, they comply until compliance gets inconvenient.

## The sovereign AI context

South Korea just allocated record budget toward what it called the world's highest performance sovereign AI model. Brazil earmarked tens of millions in leftover 5G auction funds for a sovereign AI data center. Texas A&M built a sovereign compute platform that reportedly cut research costs significantly.

All of that is infrastructure movement. Good infrastructure movement. But infrastructure without governance is just expensive hardware waiting to serve the loudest voice in the room.

Here in Ada, the question I sit with is not whether a nation or community should own its compute. That answer is yes, and I have written that case enough times in this journal. The harder question is who shapes what the compute does once it is running.

If a tribal nation builds a node, trains a model on health data, and deploys it into a clinic, the people who built the node probably understood the technical requirements. The question is whether the people in the clinic, the ones who will use it every day and whose data trained it, had a hand in deciding what it optimizes for.

If they did not, the system will work until it doesn't. And when it stops working, they will route around it. Same as the Awesome lists.

## Where this breaks in practice

Participatory sensemaking is harder than it sounds, and I want to name the cost directly.

It is slower at the start. Meaningful inclusion of community voice before a decision requires time, channel, and trust. If you do not have those things, you cannot fake them. A survey is not sensemaking. A town hall where the decision is already made is not sensemaking. A working session where the people in the room genuinely shape the frame of the problem, that is sensemaking.

The payoff is that the decision requires almost no enforcement afterward. It travels on its own because the people who will implement it built it. That is the trade. Slower front end. Faster everything after.

In sovereign AI specifically, this shows up in data governance. Who decides what the model is allowed to learn? Who decides what questions it cannot answer? Who holds the override? Those are not technical questions. They are meaning questions. And the answers that hold are the ones the community arrives at together, not the ones a vendor wrote into a terms of service.

## What the AILT frame adds

The theory positions Inclusive Adaptive Capacity and participatory sensemaking together on purpose. An organization's ability to adapt is bounded by how widely it includes voices before adapting. That is not an ethical claim. It is a structural one.

When you narrow the circle of sensemaking, you narrow the range of signals the system can read. You build in blind spots at the design stage and then spend years patching them. The sovereign and rural communities I work with and think about cannot afford that cycle. The margin for a failed deployment is thin. The cost of community distrust in a health or governance system is not theoretical.

Psychological safety is the channel this travels through. People tell you what they actually see only when they believe you will not punish them for it. That is true in a corporate boardroom. It is doubly true in a community that has watched outside institutions arrive with solutions before anyone asked about the problem.

## One practice

If you are building something for a sovereign or rural community and you have not yet held a session where the community could change your mind, you are not doing participatory sensemaking. You are doing community notification.

There is a difference. The first builds a decision that lasts. The second builds a decision that waits.

Pick up enough of those small sessions, the ones where you actually revise based on what you hear, and you get something bigger than a single good decision. You get a community that trusts the system it helped build.

Pick up enough crumbs, you get a loaf of bread.
