Congress is holding hearings this week on AI chatbot safety in healthcare. The AMA raised the concern. The question on the table is whether the tools are safe enough for clinicians to trust.

That is the right surface question. It is not the deeper one.

The deeper question is whether the people using the tools feel safe enough to say when something is wrong.

Those are not the same question.

## What safety actually does

In Adaptive Inclusive Leadership Theory, psychological safety is not one of four equal constructs. It is the mediator. The channel. The other three constructs, Inclusive Adaptive Capacity, Participatory Sensemaking, and Equity-Centered Flexibility, cannot move without it.

Here is what that means in plain terms.

You can build an organization with wide inclusion. You can invite every voice to the table before a decision gets made. But if a clinic worker in a rural Indian Health Service facility does not believe she can flag a bad AI output without someone dismissing her, the inclusion is theater. The room looks open. The channel is closed.

You can run a sensemaking process where your team builds shared meaning around a new system. But if a nurse practitioner in a sovereign community clinic thinks his concern will be logged and ignored, he stops surfacing concerns. The meaning your organization made is now partial. It reflects who felt safe enough to speak, not who was in the room.

You can build flexibility into a governance structure. But if the people closest to the patient cannot push back when the flexible system bends in the wrong direction, flexibility just means the error moves faster.

Safety is not the goal. It is the infrastructure the other goals run on.

## The Congress question restated

When the AMA tells Congress that AI chatbots in healthcare need guardrails, the word safety is doing two different jobs in the same sentence and nobody is separating them.

One job: the tool should not produce harmful clinical outputs. That is a technical and regulatory question. Federal agencies can address it with standards, red-teaming, and audits.

The other job: the clinician who sees a harmful output should feel safe enough to report it immediately and loudly. That is a leadership and culture question. Federal standards cannot address it. No bill can mandate it. You have to build it.

Sovereign and rural communities have a specific version of this problem. A federally mandated AI system arrives with its own safety documentation. The documentation says it passed the benchmarks. The clinician at the window in Ada or Tuba City or Belcourt who watches it give a wrong answer has two choices: speak up or go quiet.

Whether she speaks up depends almost entirely on whether the organizational culture she works inside has built a channel that holds her voice without punishing it.

That channel is psychological safety. And right now, in most of the sovereign and rural health settings I watch closely, that channel is undersized for the load being put on it.

That is not a soft claim. It is a structural one. Safety is not the room. It is the door. Block the door, everything you built in the room stays there.

## What to build before the next system arrives

Before a community or health organization deploys another AI tool, one question is worth answering honestly: do the people closest to the patient believe their dissent will be heard?

Not believed. Not acted on immediately. Just heard without cost.

If the answer is uncertain, the technical readiness of the AI system is almost beside the point. The feedback loop that catches errors is the most important part of any AI deployment. That loop runs through the people. And the people will only keep the loop open if the culture holds it open.

Build that first. The racks can wait a week.
