A Commonwealth Fund report published this week documents AI ambient scribing spreading into community health centers in rural care settings. A doctor speaks, the model listens, the note writes itself. Clinicians get minutes back. Patients get more eye contact. The numbers on administrative burden start to move.

That is real. I am not dismissing it.

But the Commonwealth Fund data also shows something the headlines tend to skip: the implementations that held were the ones where clinical staff, front-desk workers, and in some cases patients were brought into the design conversation before the tool went live. The ones that stalled or generated resistance were the ones that arrived as a finished product from the outside.

That gap is not a technology gap. It is an inclusion gap. And it has a name in the framework I have been building through my doctoral research: Inclusive Adaptive Capacity.

## What the construct actually means

Inclusive Adaptive Capacity is one of the four core constructs in Adaptive Inclusive Leadership Theory. The plain statement of it is this: an organization's ability to adapt is bounded by how widely it includes voices before adapting.

Not during. Not after. Before.

This is counterintuitive if you think of inclusion as a values posture. It is not. It is a structural condition. When you include a wider set of voices in the sensemaking phase, you surface the friction points, the edge cases, and the trust gaps before the rollout. You do not discover them in a staff meeting six weeks after go-live.

The ambient scribing evidence fits this exactly. A rural clinic where the medical assistant was part of the pilot design conversation knows something the vendor does not: which exam rooms have patients who do not want a device in the corner, which providers will abandon the tool at the first glitch, which front-desk staff will quietly become the best trainers if you give them a reason to care.

That knowledge does not live in the software. It lives in the people who were left out of the room.

## The speed argument goes the wrong direction

Leaders who want to move fast usually see inclusion as the thing that slows them down. More voices, more meetings, more time before anything ships.

The adaptation data does not support that. It supports the opposite.

Organizations that widened the circle before making the change adapt faster after it because they already did the sensemaking. The confusion, the resistance, the workarounds, the shadow systems, the quiet sabotage, all of it gets surfaced and resolved at the design stage instead of the recovery stage. Recovery is expensive. Recovery in a rural clinic with a staff of twelve and no IT department is a project that never finishes.

I see this in sovereign and rural communities building compute infrastructure right now. A tribe or a rural county that runs a governance conversation with its people before the first node goes in the ground will outlast the one that accepted a vendor's standard terms and started racking servers. The hardware is the same. The difference is whether the community understood what it was agreeing to, flagged the concerns that only local knowledge surfaces, and built the governing logic before the system had users depending on it.

Pick up enough crumbs, you get a loaf of bread. But you have to know which crumbs belong to your community before you start picking.

## Psychological safety is the channel, not the goal

There is a mediator sitting underneath Inclusive Adaptive Capacity that the theory names explicitly: psychological safety. Not safety as a cultural aspiration printed on a poster in the break room. Safety as the structural channel through which the other conditions travel.

If a clinical assistant does not feel safe naming the problem she sees with the ambient scribing interface, the problem does not get named. If a tribal council member does not feel safe pushing back on a compute contract because the developer is in the room and holds the relationship, the concern does not surface until it becomes a breach.

The leader's job is not to tell people it is safe to speak. It is to design conditions where speaking is actually safer than staying quiet. That is an architecture problem, not a motivational one.

## What this means for the AI rollouts coming to rural health now

STAT reported this week that some leaders in rural health are skeptical of the AI promises coming from Washington. They have reasons for that skepticism. They have watched broadband promises land as half-built infrastructure. They have watched telehealth expansions expire with the emergency declarations that funded them.

The leaders who will get something durable out of this wave are the ones who bring their staff, their patients, and their community governance structures into the design conversation now, before the contracts are signed and the tools are live. Not because that is the kind thing to do. Because that is what produces an organization capable of adapting when the first version of the tool does not work the way the demo showed.

Inclusive Adaptive Capacity is not a soft skill. It is a load-bearing wall.

Building it takes longer on day one. It is the only thing that holds when the structure gets tested.
