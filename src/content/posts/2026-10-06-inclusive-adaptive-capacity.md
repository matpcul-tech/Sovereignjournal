Five rural hospitals in West Virginia are receiving AI health care technology, according to WV News. That is a real infrastructure win. More compute closer to the patient. Faster triage support. Fewer gaps in a state where the gaps have been wide for a long time.

But infrastructure is not adaptation. And speed is not the same as fit.

Here is the construct I want to name today: **Inclusive Adaptive Capacity**. It is one of the four core constructs in Adaptive Inclusive Leadership Theory, and it says something simple that most deployment timelines ignore. An organization's ability to adapt is bounded by how widely it includes voices before adapting.

Not after. Before.

## The faster path that costs more later

When a system rolls out to five hospitals at once, there is a standard playbook. Vendor demos. IT onboarding. Staff training. Go-live date. The assumption underneath the playbook is that the technology will adapt to the community once it lands. That the nurses, the front-desk clerks, the community health workers, the patients who speak a language the model was not primarily trained on, they will all be absorbed into the workflow.

Sometimes they are. More often, the system bends the community toward the tool instead of the tool toward the community.

That is not a technology failure. It is a leadership sequence failure. The circle was too narrow at the design stage, so the adaptation engine was already underpowered before the first patient record touched it.

RFD-TV ran a piece this week on the pros and cons of AI for health information in rural communities. The tension they name is real: AI can extend reach, but it can also deliver confident-sounding answers that do not fit the specific conditions of a specific place. A patient in McDowell County, West Virginia has different infrastructure, different cultural context, different trust history with medical systems than a patient in a suburb of Columbus. A model that was not shaped by anyone who knew that difference will not close the gap. It will generate plausible-sounding content that lands wrong.

## What widening the circle actually does

Inclusive Adaptive Capacity is not a diversity metric. It is a speed metric.

Leaders who include more voices before a system is designed are buying future adaptation speed. The community health worker who flags that the patient portal does not render correctly on a prepaid phone is not slowing the project down. She is preventing a failure that would have taken six months to diagnose after go-live. The tribal health director who notes that the informed consent language does not map to how her community understands data ownership is not being difficult. She is identifying a governance gap that a class action could later walk through.

Every voice excluded at the design stage is a friction point that shows up later, at the worst possible moment, when the system is already live and politically defended.

The organizations that adapt fastest are the ones that did the inclusion work early, when it was cheap, before the architecture was locked.

## The sovereign version of this problem

At Sovereign Shield, the sovereign and rural communities we build for often carry a specific version of this problem. They are the last to be consulted and the first to be deployed on. A vendor wins a federal contract, the contract covers rural or tribal health, and the community gets a working session after the contract is signed, not before.

The governance layer matters here. If the community does not control the data, the keys, and the feedback loop, then the AI system they received is adapting to the vendor's training distribution, not to their actual patient population. The technology improves itself on data that came from somewhere else. The local edge case, the one that most needs solving, remains an edge case forever.

This is why sovereignty and inclusion are not separate conversations. A community that holds its own keys can also control what the system learns. It can close the loop between the unusual local case and the next version of the model. That is Inclusive Adaptive Capacity with infrastructure behind it.

## What the leader does

If you are managing one of those five West Virginia hospitals, or any rural or sovereign system taking on AI right now, the question worth asking before the vendor training is: who was in the room when this tool was built, and are those the same people who will be using it?

If the answer is no, you are not getting a pre-adapted tool. You are getting raw material that still needs shaping. The shaping work falls to you. That means convening your community health workers, your interpreters, your front-desk staff, your patient advisory board, before the workflows are locked, not after.

West Virginia's five hospitals getting AI infrastructure is a good development. Whether that infrastructure serves those communities well in five years depends on decisions being made right now, in rooms that are either wide or narrow.

Pick up enough crumbs, you get a loaf of bread. But you have to know which crumbs to pick up first.
