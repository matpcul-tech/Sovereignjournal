A Commonwealth Fund report published this week found that community health centers using AI ambient scribing are seeing real workflow relief, but the gains are concentrated where clinicians already had a say in how the tool got deployed.

That sentence is worth sitting with.

Not where the tool was best. Where the people using it were consulted first.

## The construct AILT names for this

Inclusive Adaptive Capacity is the first core construct in Adaptive Inclusive Leadership Theory. The claim is not soft. It is structural: an organization's ability to adapt is bounded by how widely it included voices before it had to adapt.

Bounded. As in a hard ceiling, not a preference.

Most leaders treat inclusion as a values statement. Something you put in a strategic plan or a hiring slide. AILT treats it as infrastructure. The same way a node has to be in place before data can route through it, the circle has to be wide before the signal can travel.

When the scribing tool landed in clinics where staff had no seat at the design table, the adaptation was slower, patchier, and the trust problem compounded the technical problem. That is not a morale issue. That is an architecture issue.

## What this looks like on the ground

I work with sovereign and rural communities. In that work, I have watched this play out in contexts that have nothing to do with AI. A tribal housing authority tries to retrofit a federal process. A rural county tries to adopt a broadband framework written for metro markets. The pattern is the same. The communities that move fastest are not the ones that move first. They are the ones that had the right people in the room before the decision arrived.

The ambient scribing finding is just the latest data point. STAT published a piece this week noting that some rural health leaders are skeptical of the AI-will-save-us framing coming from Washington. That skepticism is not technophobia. It is the lived experience of communities that have been adapted at, not adapted with.

There is a difference.

## The equity test

AILT has a second construct that runs alongside Inclusive Adaptive Capacity: Equity-Centered Flexibility. It holds that flexibility that does not center equity redistributes advantage to whoever was already ahead.

Apply that to AI scribing. If the tool reduces documentation burden, that is real time returned to the clinician. But if the clinician was already stretched thin because the clinic serves a Medicaid-heavy population with complex social needs, and the tool was designed against a suburban primary care workflow, the flexibility compounds the gap rather than closing it.

You adapted. You just adapted in a direction that widened the distance between the well-resourced clinic and the under-resourced one.

This is why the circle has to be wide before the adaptation starts. Not so everyone feels heard. So the adaptation goes in the right direction when it goes fast.

Pick up enough crumbs, you get a loaf of bread.

## The question for your context

If you are building something right now, in health, in infrastructure, in governance, in AI, ask yourself who was in the design conversation six months before launch. Not the stakeholder meeting. The design conversation. The one where the shape of the thing was still negotiable.

If the answer is a small room, your adaptive ceiling is set. You can still build. But you are building with a known constraint, and leadership is knowing the bill before you pay it.
